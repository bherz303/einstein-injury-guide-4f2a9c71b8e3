Injury Reporting

When an injury will keep someone off their next shift, it gets reported — same day, every time. What to do in the first hour, what happens next, and who owns which part.

00Start here

Somebody just told you they got hurt

  1. Are they safe right now? If it is serious — call for help first. Everything on this page can wait; that cannot.
  2. Ask whether it will limit their ability to work their next shift. That answer decides whether there is a report at all — see the card below.
  3. If yes, ask the one question in section 01 to land on Minor or Critical.
  4. If Critical: call Anne / Employee Experience before you send them anywhere. Pre-authorization is what keeps Concentra from turning them away or calling the office.
  5. Fill out the form today. wkf.ms/4bd5vEY — bookmark it on your desktop. You do not need the full story.
  6. Tell your RM. Injuries travel team → management → leadership, every time.

What actually gets reported

"Will this limit your ability to work your next shift?" Ask it plainly, and tell them a yes means you report it.

Yes → it gets reported. No → they work like normal and there is no report. That is the whole test.

This is not a "report everything" process, and it is not meant to be. A report has teeth: it takes the person off the schedule and puts them on leave in Rippling for two days. Filing one on someone who is perfectly able to work would pull a healthy mover off the schedule, unpaid, for no reason.

So: a cut that takes a bandaid and no time off does not get reported. Neither does a bump someone shrugs off and works through. What we are trying to catch is the injury that is going to cost them a shift — the one that used to go unrecorded until it turned into something bigger.

And the other half of the deal: an injury that is never mentioned the day it happens does not excuse the next day's absence. Hurt on the job, said nothing, then calls out for it — that is still a call out. Longstanding rule, unchanged, and it is what makes the same-day ask fair rather than one-sided.

01The one question

Everything downstream hangs on this. There are only two designations.

Does this require them to go to a medical facility the day of the injury?

MINOR

No. Small cuts, strains, aches, bruises, bangs. Nothing that needs professional medical attention or a facility visit that day.

CRITICAL

Yes. Urgent medical care needed now, or anything that puts them in a medical facility on the day it happened.

Note the line is the facility visit, not how bad it looks. A wrist that seems minor but gets driven to Concentra that afternoon is Critical. That is the call that trips people up, and it is the one that decides whether a workers' comp claim opens that day.

02Minor — the two-day path

Rest, check in, and either close it or escalate it. No drifting.

  1. File the report the same day. A follow-up pulse lands in your branch board Inbox within seconds, named for the injured person, so your branch has its own record of it.
  2. Give them up to two calendar days of rest and recovery — off the schedule. Up to; not automatically the full window. Two calendar days after the day the injury is reported. Reported Tuesday → the decision is due by end of Thursday. Calendar days, not scheduled shifts, so it does not matter how many times they were on the schedule in between.
  3. The Minor designation alerts Employee Experience to put them on leave in Rippling. That pauses their functions — they cannot work during the rest period. You do not have to request it; filing the report is what triggers it.
  4. Check in with them. You only need verbal confirmation they are ready to come back. No note, no clearance form.
  5. If they confirm at or inside the two days → close the incident report. Your confirmation is what gets them reactivated in Rippling — Employee Experience turns them back on once you say they are good. Until you do that, they stay paused and cannot be scheduled.
  6. If at the end of day two they are still in pain or still not ready → this becomes CRITICAL. See section 04.

Why the window exists. We were letting people sit out indefinitely on injuries nobody had written down — bad for them, bad for the branch, and impossible to manage. Forty-eight hours is long enough for a real strain to settle and short enough that we find out quickly when it is something more. The point of the window is the decision at the end of it, not the rest itself.

03Critical — the same-day path

Care first, then the call to Anne, then the paperwork. And the Texas Mutual sentence, every time.

  1. Take care of your person. If it is an emergency, call for help before anything else.
  2. Call Anne / Employee Experience right away — before they arrive. We pre-authorize the visit. Without it, the clinic calls the office mid-visit or can refuse to treat them. This is the step most worth remembering on this page.
  3. Send them to Concentra. Default to Concentra wherever you can — it is in the WorkWell network and it is the only place we can pre-authorize.
  4. 🔴 Whoever transports them says it out loud at the desk: "This is a workers' compensation case and it needs to be billed to Texas Mutual." Say it at every facility — Concentra, another urgent care, an ER. This is the step that protects the employee when there is no pre-auth, and there often is not.
  5. Fill out the form. Same day, with what you know. A follow-up pulse lands in your branch board Inbox here too.
  6. The workers' comp claim starts that day. Employee Experience drives it from there.

Coming back: "cleared WITHOUT restrictions" or they do not come back

A medical report that says "cleared with restrictions" is not a clearance to work. We do not offer light duty, so there is nothing for a restricted person to come back to. Restricted means not cleared for our work.

They stay off the schedule until the paperwork says cleared WITHOUT restrictions. That is the only thing that puts them back on the schedule and moves them off the claim.

This is the one most likely to get read wrong. A note that says "cleared" anywhere on it looks like a green light, especially when you are short-handed and the person wants to work. Read the whole line. If the word restrictions appears, they are not coming back yet — and putting them on the schedule anyway is how a manageable claim becomes a much worse one.

If you see a "Re-employability Program" or a light-duty role referenced in other material, that is not in practice today. It has not been finalised or confirmed. When it is real you will get proper guidance on it — until then there is no modified-duty option to offer, and nothing changes about the rule above.

Pre-authorization only exists at Concentra — read this before you need it

Concentra is the only hub we have access to for pre-authorization. Other urgent cares would prefer one too, but we do not have access to those networks, so there is nothing to call ahead for. Concentra is also inside the WorkWell network, which is why we prioritise getting people seen there.

After hours, the pre-auth question is usually moot — Concentra is likely closed, so there is nothing to authorize. Do not delay care waiting on it.

What replaces it is the sentence above: whoever is transporting the person explicitly tells the facility it is a workers' comp case that bills Texas Mutual. That is what makes the visit chargeable to the carrier rather than landing on the employee, and it works at an ER or an after-hours urgent care where a pre-auth was never possible. (Anne Bosse, 9/24/2026.)

Where the report goes when you hit submit

Both designations route the same way, automatically, within seconds:

  • Employee Experience gets it — they own the claim and the medical follow-up.
  • Your branch board Inbox gets a follow-up pulse named for the injured person.
  • Your regional manager sees it.

The branch pulse is deliberate. If something stalls on the Employee Experience side, your branch still has its own record to work from. Triage it like any other Inbox item — it arrives with no owner and no due date on purpose, so it is yours to assign.

04When Minor becomes Critical

A Minor injury that has not resolved by the end of the two days is reclassified Critical. It is the same process as section 03, just two days later:

  • Tag Anne and your regional manager on the injury pulse on your branch board, saying the injury is being upgraded to Critical. You do not change the severity yourself — we change it on the OSHA board.
  • Call Anne for the pre-authorization before they go.
  • Send them to Concentra.
  • The workers' comp claim starts that day.

This is not a failure or a black mark — it is the system working. The two days told us something a same-day guess could not.

Also fill these when they apply

  • First day absent — if you are pulling them from the field because of the injury, this is the day of the injury.
  • Witnesses — first and last name. Memories go fuzzy fast and witnesses leave the company.

Note that "which branch" and "your name" are two separate fields. That is deliberate — any manager can file for any branch. If you are the one who hears about it, you are the one who files it, even if it is not your branch and not your crew.

The "what happened" box

Write the events leading up to it, the actual injury, and why it happened. A year from now, when someone is asking questions about this claim, this paragraph is the entire record. There is an example on the form itself.

Not enough — "Hurt his back lifting."

Enough — "Employee was carrying a dresser down the customer's interior stairs with a co-pilot. Four steps from the bottom the co-pilot lost his grip, the weight shifted onto the employee, and he felt a sharp pull in his lower back on the left side. He stopped work immediately and told me within ten minutes."

The difference is not length. It is that the second one names what led up to it, who else was there, the specific injury and side, and when it was reported.

There is a direct payoff for writing it well. Employee Experience files the first injury report and the DW3 wage statement with Texas Mutual off what you wrote. In Anne's words: "the more detailed and complete the injury reporting form is, the less questions we have to ask you." Five extra minutes here is the difference between a clean handoff and a week of back-and-forth.

You do not need the whole story

Submit what you know today. Employee Experience fills in the medical and return-to-work details afterward. Waiting until you have everything is exactly the habit we are ending — a complete report filed a week late is worth less than a partial one filed the same day.

06Worked examples

Real shapes, anonymized. The call is in bold.

1. The strain that resolves

A mover tweaks his lower back loading a truck. Sore, walking fine, does not want to see anybody.

Minor.

Reported Tuesday, so the decision is due by end of Thursday. Off the schedule meanwhile. You call him Thursday morning, he says he is good — close the report, back on the schedule.

2. The strain that does not

Same start. At the day-two check-in he says it is still bothering him and he does not want to lift.

Escalates to Critical.

Tag Anne and your RM on the branch pulse to upgrade it, call Anne for the pre-auth, send him to Concentra. The workers' comp claim starts that day. Do not give him "another couple of days."

3. The one that looks minor and isn't

A mover clips his wrist hard with a dolly. It is sore but he finishes the job. That afternoon he goes to Concentra to get it looked at.

Critical — from the start.

He went to a facility the day of the injury. It does not matter that it read as a small thing in the moment. The claim should open that day, and Anne should have been called before he went. This exact case happened, and it was logged as Minor. It is the most common way to get this wrong.

4. The one that was already hurting

A mover has been favoring his shoulder for a week. On a job he lifts wrong and it gets much worse.

Report it, and describe both.

Write the pre-existing complaint and the new incident in the "what happened" box, plainly. Do not decide which one caused it — that is a medical and claims question, and guessing at it in writing creates a problem for everyone. Your job is an accurate account.

5. The one that does not get reported

A mover cuts his hand on a box strap. He puts a bandaid on it and keeps working. He is on the schedule tomorrow and has no issue with it.

No report.

You still ask: "Is this going to limit your ability to work your next shift?" He says no, and he works the next day. That is the end of it. Filing here would take a healthy mover off the schedule for two unpaid days over a bandaid.

Same cut, different answer: he says it is deep, his grip is weak, and he does not want to lift tomorrow. That is a report. The injury did not change — his answer did, and his answer is the thing that decides it.

6. The late mention

On Thursday a mover mentions his elbow has hurt since Monday's job.

File it Thursday.

Treat it exactly as if it happened today. It gets the same urgency, the same form, the same two-day window — the age of the injury does not discount it.

Date of injury = Monday. Date reported = Thursday. The form has separate fields for exactly this, and the gap between them is real information, not something to hide. Say so plainly in the description — something like "minor injury occurred a few days ago, was not reported at the time, and the injury has since gotten worse." That sentence is what makes the record make sense a year from now.

Note what makes this different from a call out: he worked those days and is telling you now. If instead he had said nothing and then called out Thursday because of Monday's injury, the absence is a call out — see the FAQ. The injury still gets filed either way; it is the missed shift that is treated differently.

7. The emergency

A mover falls off the back of the truck and cannot put weight on his ankle.

Critical. Care first, paperwork after.

Get him help — 911 if it warrants it. Then your BM, then your RM, then Anne. The form gets filled out once he is being taken care of, not before.

07Who owns what

You — the manager on duty

  • Make sure they are safe, and make the Minor / Critical call
  • Call Anne before sending anyone to urgent care
  • File the report the same day, with what you know
  • Run the two-day window on a Minor: rest, check in, close or escalate
  • Give Employee Experience the word when they are ready — nobody comes off leave until you do
  • On a claim, do not schedule anyone back without "cleared without restrictions" in writing — "cleared with restrictions" is not a clearance
  • Cover the schedule — without pressuring anyone back early
  • Keep talking to them. Nobody should be sitting at home hearing nothing from us
  • Answer Employee Experience's follow-up questions fast. Slow answers are what make claims drag

Employee Experience (Anne)

  • Pre-authorizes the urgent care visit — that is why you call first
  • Puts the mover on leave in Rippling when a Minor is filed, and reactivates them once you confirm they are ready
  • Owns the workers' comp claim end to end
  • Submits to Texas Mutual on your behalf — the first injury report and the DW3 wage statement. Completing the form is what hands that off; you do not file anything with the carrier yourself
  • Fills in the medical and return-to-work detail on the report
  • Handles the carrier, the OSHA recordkeeping, and benefits questions
  • Guides restrictions and the return-to-work conversation

What happens on the pay side — and what to say when they ask

  • Temporary income benefits start after 7 days off, at up to 70% of average weekly wage, paid through Texas Mutual — not through us.
  • If they are out 15 or more days, backpay applies.
  • After about a month on the workers' comp program, Anne or Jalissa takes over communication with the injured employee directly.

You can tell them those three things. Anything beyond it — their specific amount, their specific dates, whether a given week is covered — goes to Employee Experience. Do not estimate someone's benefit for them. (Anne Bosse, 9/24/2026.)

Your Regional Manager

  • Gets told about every reported injury — the chain is team → management → leadership, every time
  • Is your partner on the day-two escalation call if you are unsure
  • Backs you on coverage so nobody feels pushed to come back early
  • Escalates when something is not moving

08What your crew needs to know

None of this works if the crew does not tell you in the first place.

Deliver this out loud, not just on a board

  • At a huddle, once, to everybody — and then again with every new hire during onboarding.
  • Say it yourself. Pinning it to a wall does not do the job. The thing that makes someone speak up is having heard their own manager say it is fine to.
  • The tone carries more than the words. If a crew believes reporting an injury makes them a problem, they will work through it and tell you in two weeks when it is worse. That is the outcome this whole process exists to prevent.
  • The mechanics are deliberately not in the message. Time off the schedule, the Rippling leave and what is or is not paid get handled person by person, when it is actually happening. Put them in a broadcast and a safety message starts reading like an offer.
  • The call-out line is the part to say clearly. It is not a threat — it is the reason the same-day ask is fair. We can only cover someone who told us.

The message — as posted

Hey team,

We have an updated policy and best practice around injuries and getting them reported. Here is what we need from you.

If you have an injury that is serious enough that you can not work your next shift it needs to be communicated the day of the injury. DO NOT WAIT.

Tell your manager the day it happens.
Not the next morning. The day it happens. That one step is what lets us get you covered and get you care — we cannot do either for an injury nobody told us about.

This has always been our rule and it has not changed: if you get hurt on the job, do not say anything to a manager that day, and then call out the next day because of that injury — it still counts as a call out. Communicating clearly to us when it happens is what changes that.

Management has guidelines for reporting and getting you taken care of: a report will be filed and management will advise you on next steps as needed.

Critical injuries are obvious: that instance will be communicated of course and we will get you to a care facility right away so no need to worry. If it is an injury that is not critical but still serious enough to limit your ability to work, then make sure that is communicated before end of day.

You do not get in trouble for reporting an injury. The only thing that causes a problem is an injury nobody knew about.

Of course, let us avoid injuries and take care of ourselves and each other out in the field — but if something happens, communicate clearly to your management team.

09FAQ

He got hurt yesterday, said nothing, and called out today because of it.

It still counts as a call out. That has always been our rule and it has not changed. Getting hurt on the job does not retroactively excuse an absence that was never communicated — telling us the day it happens is the thing that changes it from a call out into a managed injury.

Two separate things, and do both:

  • Attendance: the absence is recorded as a call out, handled the way you handle any other.
  • The injury: you still file the report now. An unreported injury does not become acceptable because the reporting was late — see "The late mention" in section 06.

Say the rule out loud when it happens, calmly, so the next one gets reported on the day. The point is not to penalise the injury — it is that we cannot cover someone who did not tell us.

He mentioned something but says he's fine. Do I still file?

Say this to him, out loud:

"If this is going to limit your ability to work your next shift, then I'm going to report it — and that takes you off the schedule. If you're telling me you're good, then I'm expecting you to work like normal."

Then take his answer. If it will cost him a shift — he needs off a task, off the schedule, or he is favouring it — file it. If he says he is fine, he works like normal and no report is needed.

A cut he puts a bandaid on and works through the next day is not a report. That is the case this process is deliberately not trying to capture.

Why it is phrased that way: the call is his, not yours. You are not judging how bad the injury is — you are making sure he knows what mentioning it means, so that if he needs something he says so, and if he says he is good, he has said it knowing what that means.

Do I need to know how bad it is before I file?

No, and waiting to find out is the habit this replaces. File the same day with what you know. Employee Experience fills in the rest.

His doctor's note says "cleared with restrictions." Can he work?

No. We do not offer light duty, so there is no restricted work to give him. "Cleared with restrictions" means he is not cleared for our work.

He stays off the schedule until a report says "cleared WITHOUT restrictions." That is the only thing that returns him to the schedule and moves him off the claim.

Read the whole line before you act on it. A note with the word "cleared" on it looks like a green light — especially when you are short-handed and he wants to work. If the word restrictions is in there, he is not back yet.

Does the same paperwork rule apply to a Minor two-day rest?

No — those are two different paths.

  • Minor, no facility visit: verbal confirmation is all you need. No note, no form.
  • Anything on a claim, or anyone who was seen by a doctor: written clearance, and it has to say without restrictions.

Do not start asking for paperwork on a two-day rest, and do not accept a verbal "I am good" from someone who has been to a clinic.

What does Employee Experience actually do once I have filed it?

They take over the carrier side. Once a member of branch leadership completes the injury reporting form, Employee Experience submits to Texas Mutual — the first injury report and the DW3 wage statement. You do not file anything with the carrier yourself.

They also own the claim from there: the medical follow-up, restrictions, the return-to-work conversation, and the OSHA recordkeeping.

What they need from you is a complete form. The detail you write is what they submit — thin answers come back to you as questions. (Anne Bosse, 9/24/2026.)

Does filing the form count as telling Anne?

For a Minor, the report is the notification. For a Critical, no — call her. The pre-authorization has to happen before the person gets to urgent care, and a form submission is not a phone call.

What if it happens on a weekend or after hours?

Weekends are normal working days for this. We usually have managers on over the weekend, and any manager can fill out the form for any branch — you do not have to be that mover's manager, or even at that branch. The form asks which branch separately from who is filling it out. So file it; do not hold it for Monday and do not go hunting for the "right" person.

After hours and Critical is the one exception. Get the team member to the care facility they need and get it handled. Then fill out the form the next day when you are back in the office. Care first — do not let paperwork, or not being able to reach anyone, slow down getting them seen.

There is no after-hours pre-authorization to chase — pre-auth exists at Concentra only, and after hours Concentra is likely closed. Instead: whoever takes them says at the desk that it is a workers' comp case billed to Texas Mutual. That sentence is what protects them. Tell Anne as soon as you can reach her.

Concentra is closed or too far. Can they go somewhere else?

Yes — get them care. Concentra is the default because it is in the WorkWell network and the only place we can pre-authorize, but a closed clinic is not a reason to delay treatment.

At any other facility, the sentence does the work: whoever transports them tells the desk "this is a workers' compensation case and it needs to be billed to Texas Mutual." There is no pre-auth to arrange at those places — we do not have access to their networks — so saying it explicitly is the whole protection.

Then tell Anne as soon as you can reach her.

Can they go to their own doctor instead of Concentra?

Not first. They need to be seen at one of our in-network facilities associated with our workers' compensation coverage.

A second opinion is allowed — but only after they have been seen by our workers' comp doctors. Their own physician is not the first stop.

What if the employee refuses treatment?

If the injury is keeping them off the schedule, refusing treatment is not an option we can accept.

The reason is not paperwork. An injury that goes unchecked can develop into something worse, and an undocumented one leaves the employee without the protection the claim exists to give them. If they are hurt enough not to work, they are hurt enough to be seen.

If someone pushes back, do not negotiate it on your own — call your RM and Anne the same day, and write in the report that treatment was offered and declined.

Are the rest days paid?

No. Rest days outside a workers' comp claim are not paid days.

If it escalates to Critical and a claim opens, wage replacement is handled through the claim and Employee Experience runs it. Route any pay question to them rather than answering it yourself.

How long exactly is the rest window, and when does it start?

Two calendar days after the day the injury is reported. Reported Monday → the decision is due by end of Wednesday.

Calendar days, not scheduled shifts — it does not matter how many times they were on the schedule in between. A report that comes in late in the afternoon still gets both full days.

He already went to Concentra before telling me. Minor or Critical?

Critical. The question is whether he went to a facility on the day of the injury, not whether you were the one who sent him. Tell Anne immediately — the visit was not pre-authorized.

I already filed it as Minor and it turned out to be worse.

Tag Anne and your regional manager on the injury pulse on your branch board and say the injury is being upgraded to Critical. We change it on the OSHA board — you do not need to go edit the record yourself.

It works the same the other way. If you marked it Critical and it turns out to be Minor, do exactly the same thing and we will change it.

Either direction is the designed path, not a mistake. Getting the designation wrong at first is expected; leaving it wrong is the only real error.

Is there such a thing as an injury I note but do not file?

No. That category is gone. There are no near misses, no injury notes, no "I'll keep an eye on it."

Once someone tells you an injury will keep them from working their next shift, you file it. No matter what. There is no version where you note it, watch it, and decide later. That judgment call is not yours and it no longer exists.

That is different from a passing mention — see "He mentioned something but says he's fine" above. The dividing line is his answer, not your read of how bad it looks.

And if someone gets hurt and never tells management at all, we cannot act on it and it goes on as is.

10What not to do

  • Do not wait to file until you have the full story, a diagnosis, or a quiet moment.
  • Do not send anyone to urgent care without calling Anne first.
  • Do not decide what caused an injury in writing — describe what happened and let the medical and claims side do their work.
  • Do not let a Minor drift past the two days without a decision. Drifting is the thing this replaces — and it now has a cost attached: they are paused in Rippling until you confirm, so a check-in you forget is someone sitting at home unable to work and unpaid.
  • Do not talk anyone out of reporting once they have told you it will cost them a shift. Asking the question is not the same as arguing them down from the answer.
  • Do not put anyone back on the schedule on a "cleared with restrictions" note. That is not a clearance — we have no light duty to give them. It has to say without restrictions.
  • Do not promise anything about pay, benefits, or time off. Route it to Employee Experience.
  • Do not use nicknames on the form. It becomes a claim record.
